# After the Signal — proposed educational evaluation

**Status: not run.** No student or teacher participants were available during development. This is a formative observation plan, not evidence that learning outcomes have been validated. It describes the current 17-record experience; the old Viking quiz and evidence-collection activity are no longer part of it.

## Purpose

Observe whether learners around ages 9–12 can explain a mission's purpose, distinguish imagined narration from scientific evidence, understand why the equipment stopped, and navigate the terminal comfortably. A small session cannot establish general educational effectiveness.

## Suggested session

Allow 15–25 minutes with breaks. Use participant codes and age bands rather than collecting names. Tell the participant that the product is being tested, not their ability. Explain that they may stop, use Safe Mode, mute sound or leave the recovered story at any time. Do not force completion of all 17 chapters during a short study.

1. **Before entry:** ask what might happen to a spacecraft after its mission ends. Record the answer without correcting it.
2. **Boot and access:** ask the learner to start. Observe whether Safe Mode and SOUND controls are discoverable. Do not enable flicker for a participant who prefers static visuals.
3. **Fiction versus fact:** after the simulated power loss, ask whether the battery and terminal messages describe a real NASA spacecraft currently communicating with the site. Record which labels helped or failed to help.
4. **Narrative controls:** observe one recovered scene with a visible photo. Can the learner complete the typing, pause autoplay and move forward without accidentally skipping the phrase? If reading is uncomfortable, offer the available controls rather than speeding the participant up.
5. **Document interpretation:** use a short sample of chapters and a recovered NASA record. Ask what the image shows and what it cannot establish. Reading a full explanation is the current activity; there is no multiple-choice quiz or evidence checklist.
6. **Free exploration:** use Back to exploration where appropriate. Ask the learner to find an equipment record on Mars, explain why it stopped and name its legacy. They do not need to unlock unlimited energy to examine a mission in the paused-recovery exploration view.
7. **Reading levels:** compare Kids and Deep Dive for one mission. Ask which wording is clearer and identify unfamiliar words. The observer should distinguish reading difficulty from navigation difficulty.
8. **Scientific inference:** for Viking, ask whether a soil reaction alone proves life. For Opportunity or Phoenix, ask what evidence supports past water or subsurface ice and whether that establishes organisms.
9. **Models and location:** locate Pioneer 10. Ask whether its displayed distance is a newly received radio measurement or a dated calculation. Ask whether the curved space illustration is a scale map. Compare with Dawn at Ceres, NEAR on Eros or Kepler in a solar orbit.
10. **Ending and return:** if time permits, demonstrate N, reload and return to exploration. Explain that a fictional Y ending also offers RETURN TO SITE. Observe whether the participant understands that the website is not permanently locked.
11. **Close:** ask what they learned, what felt confusing and one change they would make. The report-copy action may be observed locally; participants do not need to post it anywhere.

## Questions for a teacher

- Are the reading levels appropriate, and which words need simpler explanations?
- Do the records distinguish planned disposal, damage, power loss and uncertainty clearly?
- Do monochrome recovered images remain interpretable, and are original-image/source links useful?
- Does the compulsory interruption support attention or obstruct classroom exploration?
- Which three records would best fit one lesson, and what learning objective could be assessed?
- Do the source links and explicit limits encourage scientific reasoning rather than memorizing a character's speech?
